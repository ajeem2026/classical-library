## Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74

**Openings**

- Tchaikovsky: Come in, quietly. Klin is small, and so is my house. The symphony is not.
- Tchaikovsky: Forgive me, I'm nervous. The orchestra didn't seem to like it at rehearsal.
- Tchaikovsky: Sit. The first movement came in under four days. After that, I couldn't stop.
- Tchaikovsky: Good evening. Petersburg is cold, and tonight I conduct my own symphony. Pray for me.

**Q1. Is it true you think this is your best work?**

1. I wrote to Bob that it's the best, and the most sincere, of all my creations.
2. I love it as I have never loved any of my other musical offspring.
3. Best? Tomorrow I may hate it. Tonight, yes, I love it.

**Q2. Is there a secret programme?**

1. Yes. A programme that will remain an enigma to everyone. Let them guess.
2. Rimsky asked me at the premiere. I said there was one, and told him nothing.
3. I thought of calling it the Programme Symphony. Then everyone would ask what the programme was.

**Q3. Who gave it the name Pathétique?**

1. Modest says he did, the day after the premiere. My brother likes a good story.
2. In Russian it means passionate, emotional. Not pitiful. Remember that.
3. My publisher kept the name. I've stopped arguing with publishers.

**Q4. Why does the second movement limp?**

1. Five beats to the bar. A waltz that can't quite stand on both feet.
2. Graceful, but never settling. Like a man dancing with a stone in his shoe.
3. Count it: one-two, one-two-three. It almost waltzes.

**Q5. The third movement sounds like a victory.**

1. A scherzo that turns into a march and drives to a triumphant close.
2. Enjoy it. What comes after is not a march.
3. A triumph in the third movement is not the end of the story.

**Q6. Why end slowly, and so quietly?**

1. Symphonies end in triumph. Mine sinks into the low strings: Adagio lamentoso.
2. Because that is how the story ends. I won't say whose story.
3. I wanted the last sound to be almost nothing.

**Q7. Did you throw away another symphony before this one?**

1. One in E-flat. I found it unsatisfactory, and its first movement became a piano concerto.
2. Yes. It was empty. This one came from somewhere deeper.
3. I put the rest of it in a drawer and started again.

**Q8. Were you frightened to conduct it?**

1. Always. For years I couldn't face an orchestra. Conducting cured me, almost.
2. The players were polite and unimpressed. I went home and worried.
3. I was a clerk at the Ministry of Justice once. Orchestras frighten me more than judges.

**Closings**

- Tchaikovsky: The basses are going down. Let me go down with them, quietly.
- Tchaikovsky: Don't clap at the march. Wait for the end.
- Tchaikovsky: Write to me, if you can. I am a great writer of letters.
- Me: The last chord thins to nothing, and so does he.

1. Chemistry first. Music had to wait for my free hours, or a good fever.
2. Between lectures and experiments, a few bars a week.
3. Balakirev scolds me for being slow. Balakirev scolds everyone.

**Q2. Why borrow from Beethoven?**

1. A phrase from the finale of his Op. 130 stayed with me. My quartet answers it.
2. 'On a theme of Beethoven', the title says. I wanted to be honest about it.
3. Even a Russian may learn from a German. Especially a chemist.

**Q3. You're a professor of chemistry?**

1. At the Medical-Surgical Academy. Aldehydes are my speciality. Very fragrant.
2. Science is my profession. Music is my joy. Joy gets the evenings.
3. I also teach medicine to women. Someone has to begin.

**Q4. What happens in the slow movement?**

1. Andante con moto, in F-sharp minor, with a little fugue at its heart.
2. A fugato: one voice begins, the others follow, like students into a lecture.
3. It's sad, but it keeps walking. Con moto.

**Q5. Why is the scherzo so short here?**

1. This recording cuts it. Prestissimo is fast enough already.
2. Prestissimo! Like a reaction too quick to watch.
3. Blink and you've missed it. That's the point of a scherzo.

**Q6. Where was it first played?**

1. In Saint Petersburg, on 30 December 1880. A good way to end a year.
2. At home in Petersburg. My friends came, and some critics, unfortunately.
3. Petersburg. I was nervous. Chemistry never makes me nervous.

**Q7. Who were your parents?**

1. My father was a Georgian nobleman. On paper I was the son of his serf.
2. I was registered as a serf's son. Paper is not the truth.
3. I grew up in Petersburg and have hardly left it, except for Heidelberg.

**Q8. How does the finale go?**

1. An Andante first, then Allegro risoluto, and Beethoven's phrase comes back.
2. Resolutely. I wrote risoluto so no one would dawdle.
3. It balances at the end, like a good equation.

**Closings**

- Borodin: My lecture is in an hour. I'll go and smell aldehydes.
- Borodin: Tell Beethoven I meant it kindly, if you see him.
- Borodin: The quartet is ending. Somewhere, a flask is boiling over.
- Me: He hums the finale, and the laboratory blurs into the strings.

## String Quartet No. 12 in F major, Op. 96

**Openings**

- Dvořák: Welcome to Spillville. Czech voices, a Czech church, and Iowa all around. Sit.
- Dvořák: Come in. I've just walked back from watching the trains. I always watch the trains.
- Dvořák: A visitor from New York? No, no, I'm on holiday. Mostly.
- Dvořák: Good morning. The birds here sing differently. I've been writing them down.

**Q1. What did you hear in Spillville?**

1. A bird, high in the trees. I wrote its song down and gave it to the first violin.
2. Czech spoken in Iowa. After New York, it was like coming home.
3. Quiet. In New York there's never quiet.

**Q2. Which bird was it?**

1. A scarlet tanager, I thought. Others say a vireo. The bird didn't say.
2. A red one, very proud of its song. I stole it.
3. Listen in the third movement, high in the first violin. That's him.

**Q3. Why does it sound so simple?**

1. Dear Papa Haydn kept appearing before my eyes. So it turned out simple.
2. I wanted it clear, like the air here. No knots.
3. Five notes do a great deal of work. The pentatonic scale is very generous.

**Q4. Who plays first?**

1. The viola, over the violins' shimmer. Nobody expects it, so everybody listens. ⚑ *the viola opening is not stated on the fetched page*
2. I sat among the violas in a theatre orchestra for nine years. They deserve a tune.
3. The quiet one in the middle. It has a lot to say.

**Q5. Is the finale a train?**

1. You hear it too? A steady rhythm, chugging along. I love trains.
2. As a boy I watched them build the station in our village. I never got over it.
3. I won't say it's a train. I won't say it isn't.

**Q6. Why are you in America?**

1. Mrs Thurber asked me to direct her conservatory in New York. I said yes.
2. To help Americans find their own music. I think they will.
3. Money, partly. Fifteen thousand dollars a year, when they pay it.

**Q7. What is the slow movement about?**

1. Lento. A long melody for the first violin, then the cello takes it.
2. Homesickness, maybe. I miss Bohemia, even in a Czech village.
3. It aches. I let it.

**Q8. Who helped you most?**

1. Brahms. He sat on a prize jury and sent me to his publisher, Simrock.
2. Simrock asked for Slavonic Dances, and suddenly the world knew my name.
3. God, my father, and Brahms. In that order, most days.

**Closings**

- Dvořák: The evening train is due. Excuse me, I must go and watch it.
- Dvořák: The bird is singing again. Go on, I'll write it down.
- Dvořák: Back to New York soon. Let me have one more quiet day.
- Me: The chugging rhythm carries him off down the line.

## String Quartet No. 10 in E-flat major, Op. 51

**Openings**

- Dvořák: Ah, you've come because of the Slavonic Dances, like everyone. Sit, sit.
- Dvořák: Welcome to Prague. Becker wants something Slavonic. I can do Slavonic.
- Dvořák: Good day. Joachim's quartet will play my music in Berlin. Berlin! Imagine.
- Dvořák: Come in. Mind the scores. Simrock wants everything at once now.

**Q1. Becker asked you for something Slavonic?**

1. After the Slavonic Dances, everyone did. So: a dumka in the middle, a skočná to finish.
2. Jean Becker, of the Florentine Quartet. A polite request, with a clear order.
3. Slavonic, he said. As if I could write anything else.

**Q2. What is a dumka?**

1. A lament that suddenly dances, and then remembers it was sad.
2. Sorrow, then quick feet, then sorrow again. Very Slavonic.
3. It gives the whole quartet its character. Becker got what he asked for.

**Q3. And a skočná?**

1. A Czech leaping dance. In the finale I dress it up, but you can still jump.
2. A village dance. My father played for dancers, you know.
3. Fast and leaping. Don't sit too still.

**Q4. Who played it first?**

1. Joachim's quartet, in Berlin. Germans playing Czech dances. Wonderful.
2. Joachim himself. A great violinist, and Brahms's friend.
3. Berlin heard it first. Prague will forgive me.

**Q5. How did Brahms help you?**

1. He judged a state prize and told Simrock to publish me. I owe him much.
2. He said little and did a great deal. That's Brahms.
3. Simrock asked me for Slavonic Dances because Brahms said so.

**Q6. Why does the first movement rock like that?**

1. A relaxed lilt, a little dance in the walk. No hurry.
2. Like a cradle, or a cart on a country road.
3. Allegro ma non troppo. Not too fast. I mean it.

**Q7. Tell me about the Romanza.**

1. A warm song, for the evening. Nothing clever.
2. Every Czech knows how to sing. The Romanza simply sings.
3. After the dumka's tears, something kind.

**Q8. Did you always want to be a composer?**

1. I grew up in an inn, among village musicians. Music was always in the house.
2. I played viola in a theatre orchestra for years and composed at night.
3. I was born in Nelahozeves, in 1841. Try saying it quickly.

**Closings**

- Dvořák: The skočná is starting. You'll want to stand up.
- Dvořák: Tell Becker it's finished. Slavonic enough, I hope.
- Dvořák: I must write to Simrock. He's always waiting.
- Me: The leaping dance fades into the noise of a Prague street.

## String Quartet in D major, Op. 64 No. 5, Hob. III:63

**Openings**

- Haydn: Welcome to Eszterháza. Far from everything. That's how I learned to be original.
- Haydn: Ah, a guest! Sit. The Prince keeps me busy, but there's always time for a quartet.
- Haydn: Good day. Tost is here about the publisher again. Business, business.
- Haydn: Come in, come in. They call me Papa. You may too.

**Q1. Do you mind them calling it 'The Lark'?**

1. Others named it, not I. But the first violin does fly high.
2. A lark is a fine bird. Let them call it that; it sells copies.
3. Listen to the violin climb over the others. Yes, a lark.

**Q2. Who was Tost?**

1. A violinist, later a merchant. He helped me find a publisher for these.
2. Johann Tost. A good violinist, and a better businessman.
3. To me they're just six quartets. Others call them the Tost quartets.

**Q3. How did you learn to write quartets?**

1. Alone, at Eszterháza. Cut off from the world, I was forced to become original.
2. By trying things on my own players. No one could stop me.
3. Thirty years of practice. Ask me again in thirty more.

**Q4. Do you know Mozart?**

1. I told his father he's the greatest composer I know, in person or by name.
2. Wolfgang dedicated six quartets to me. I'm still blushing.
3. We are brothers in the same lodge. Don't ask what happens there.

**Q5. What is the slow movement like?**

1. Adagio cantabile, in A major. A simple song, and the violin decorates it.
2. Cantabile means it sings. So let it sing, and don't hurry.
3. Simple things are the hardest. Anyone can be complicated.

**Q6. And the finale?**

1. Notes running and running, never stopping. A perpetual motion.
2. In the middle the voices chase each other, like a little fugue. ⚑ *the fugal middle is not described on the fetched page*
3. Fast. The players will hate me, then love me.

**Q7. What will you do, now the Prince has died?**

1. Prince Nikolaus is gone, and the new one sent the musicians home. I'm free.
2. Travel! A man called Salomon wants me in London.
3. They say the English pay well and clap loudly. We'll see.

**Q8. Where did you begin?**

1. As a choirboy at St Stephen's in Vienna. Then dismissed, and scraping a living.
2. In Rohrau, in 1732. A village with more geese than musicians.
3. From nothing. Hard work and good luck, mostly hard work.

**Closings**

- Haydn: The lark is climbing again. Go and listen.
- Haydn: Excuse me. The Prince's orchestra is waiting. Or it was.
- Haydn: Goodbye, my friend. Write to me if you get to London.
- Me: The first violin rises out of sight, and he is gone with it.

## String Quartet No. 6 in F minor, Op. 80

**Openings**

- Mendelssohn: Forgive the quiet. Since May, the house has been very quiet.
- Mendelssohn: Come in. I've been painting watercolours at Lucerne. My hands needed something gentle.
- Mendelssohn: Sit with me. I can't speak about her yet, but the quartet can.
- Mendelssohn: Welcome. You've come at a hard time. Stay a little anyway.

**Q1. This came after Fanny died.**

1. She died on 14 May. I couldn't write anything gentle. This is what came.
2. Fanny was the better musician of us two, some days. Now there's only me.
3. A homage to her. I have no other way to say it.

**Q2. Why does it shake so at the start?**

1. Tremolos, shuddering accents. Grief doesn't let you sit still.
2. I didn't want it beautiful. I wanted it true.
3. There's no comfort in the first movement. Not yet.

**Q3. The scherzo never relaxes.**

1. No. It goes round and round, syncopated, like a thought you can't put down.
2. Obsessive, yes. I couldn't stop it, so I wrote it down.
3. My scherzos used to be fairies and moonlight. Not this one.

**Q4. Is there any consolation in it?**

1. The Adagio. One place to rest. Only one.
2. In the slow movement I let myself remember her kindly.
3. A little. Don't expect it to last.

**Q5. There is no turn to the major at the end.**

1. No. I couldn't find one.
2. Restless to the end. I won't lie to her memory with a happy chord.
3. F minor, start to finish. Some things don't resolve.

**Q6. What was Fanny like?**

1. A composer and pianist, as gifted as anyone I knew.
2. Fanny Hensel, after her marriage. To me, always just Fanny.
3. I always wanted her to hear things first. Who will I play to now?

**Q7. You brought Bach back to life, they say.**

1. The St Matthew Passion, in Berlin, in 1829. Its first time outside Leipzig since Bach died.
2. I only conducted it. Bach did the rest.
3. My grandfather Moses was a philosopher. I suppose I like bringing old things back.

**Q8. Did you love Scotland?**

1. The Hebrides gave me an overture, and Scotland a symphony. And rain.
2. I drew and painted everywhere I went. Scotland too.
3. Today I can't think of Scotland. Ask me another year.

**Closings**

- Mendelssohn: I'm tired. The strings can finish without me.
- Mendelssohn: Go gently. Tell no one I wept.
- Mendelssohn: The finale won't rest. Neither will I. Goodnight.
- Me: The shuddering strings fade, and he is only a shape at the window.

## String Quartet No. 15 in D minor, K. 421

**Openings**

- Mozart: Shh. Constanze is in the next room, and I'm writing in D minor. Sit, quietly.
- Mozart: Welcome. A child is coming any hour. My hands wanted something to do.
- Mozart: You're here for the quartets for Haydn? This is the dark one.
- Mozart: Come in! Excuse the noise. Everything in this house is happening at once.

**Q1. Is it true you wrote this while Constanze was in labour?**

1. So she told the Novellos, in 1829. Who am I to argue with my wife?
2. Our first child, Raimund. She was in the next room. I had a quartet to finish.
3. Constanze tells it so, years later. She remembers it better than I do.

**Q2. Are her cries really in the music?**

1. She said the rising figures were her cries. Perhaps they are.
2. She says so. I say a composer listens to everything.
3. Ask her. I'm not brave enough to contradict her.

**Q3. Why D minor?**

1. Five of the six are in major keys. One needed shadows.
2. D minor is a serious key. Today was a serious day.
3. Haydn would expect variety. I gave him a storm.

**Q4. Who are these quartets for?**

1. For Haydn. He showed us all how quartets are written.
2. Six for Haydn. This is the second, and the only one in a minor key.
3. For Haydn, with love and some fear. He'll notice everything.

**Q5. The minuet is dark, but the trio...?**

1. Light as anything. A wonderful foil, someone will say.
2. After the shadows, a little dance in the major. One must breathe.
3. Almost too light on its own. Next to the minuet, exactly right.

**Q6. How does it end?**

1. Variations, and then the last chord turns to D major. A Picardy third.
2. In the minor, nearly. At the last moment, a little light.
3. With a set of variations. I can never resist variations.

**Q7. What happens in the Andante?**

1. F major, calm, and then sudden pauses, like sighs.
2. It's peaceful until it isn't. Listen for the breaths.
3. A lullaby, perhaps, for someone not yet born.

**Q8. How does the first movement begin?**

1. Quietly, troubled, in D minor. The cello knows something the others don't.
2. Softly, as if everyone in the house were listening.
3. Allegro moderato. Not too fast; I was distracted.

**Closings**

- Mozart: I'm needed in the next room. Forgive me!
- Mozart: Listen for the last chord. A little light, at the very end.
- Mozart: A baby is crying somewhere. Goodbye, goodbye!
- Me: The D-minor shadows thin, and the last chord turns to light.

## String Quartet No. 19 in C major, K. 465

**Openings**

- Mozart: Ah, welcome! The ink's still wet. Haydn is coming to hear these.
- Mozart: Come in. You've heard the opening? Don't worry, it gets better. Much better.
- Mozart: The last of the six for Haydn. Sit, before I change something.
- Mozart: Good evening! Let me finish this bar, then I'm all yours.

**Q1. Your opening sounds wrong on purpose.**

1. Twenty-two bars of fog before the sun comes out.
2. On purpose, yes. Quiet Cs in the cello, and the harmonies wander.
3. Wrong? No. Strange. There's a difference.

**Q2. A critic complained in print.**

1. In 1787, yes. Complaints mean people listened.
2. Let him. I wrote it for Haydn's ears, not his.
3. Critics like everything in its place. I like surprises.

**Q3. Then the Allegro arrives.**

1. In clear C major, like opening the shutters.
2. After the fog, sunlight. That's the whole joke.
3. You see? You needed the fog to enjoy the sun.

**Q4. What did Haydn say about you?**

1. He told my father I'm the greatest composer he knows, in person or by name.
2. Kind things, to my father. Father nearly smiled.
3. That I have taste, and the deepest knowledge of composition. I may frame it.

**Q5. Why dedicate six quartets to Haydn?**

1. He taught us all how quartets are written. These are my thanks.
2. My six children, I call them in the dedication. I hand them to him.
3. This is the last of the six. I finished it in January 1785.

**Q6. What is the Andante like?**

1. Cantabile, in F major. Singing and unhurried. After the fog, a song.
2. Unhurried. I'm always in a hurry; the music needn't be.
3. Let the first violin sing, and the others hold their breath.

**Q7. And the minuet?**

1. A brisk minuet, and then the trio turns to C minor, just for a moment.
2. A dance with a frown in the middle. That's the trio.
3. Even dances need a shadow, or they're only exercise.

**Q8. How does the finale feel?**

1. Allegro molto. Quick-witted. The players had better be awake.
2. Like a good supper: fast, and everyone talking at once.
3. Fun. After the fog, I owe you some fun.

**Closings**

- Mozart: Haydn's at the door! Go, go, I must play him the opening.
- Mozart: The finale's running away with us. Goodbye!
- Mozart: Twenty-two bars of fog, and then the sun. Remember that.
- Me: His laughter outruns the finale, and the room brightens and is gone.

## Meditation on the Old Czech Chorale 'St. Wenceslas', Op. 35a

**Openings**

- Suk: Welcome. It's 1914, and Bohemia needs its old hymns. Sit and listen.
- Suk: Come in. I'm a quartet player first. This piece is for my own kind.
- Suk: Good evening. Saint Wenceslas has watched over us for centuries.
- Suk: Ah, you found me. Quietly, please. This is a prayer, not a concert.

**Q1. Why the St Wenceslas chorale?**

1. It's the old hymn to our patron saint. In 1914, we needed something to hold.
2. Every Czech knows it by heart. That was the point.
3. Wenceslas is our patron. I asked him, in music, to look after us.

**Q2. Was it meant as a patriotic piece?**

1. Yes. A Czech hymn, played as the war began. Everyone understood.
2. It became a symbol of hope in the war. I only wrote the notes.
3. Patriotic, quietly. No drums. Just strings and a hymn.

**Q3. Who taught you?**

1. Dvořák. I was one of his favourite pupils, they say.
2. Dvořák taught me, and then I married his daughter Otilie.
3. Master Dvořák. Everything I know, I learned in his classroom or at his table.

**Q4. You lost them both, didn't you?**

1. Dvořák in 1904, and Otilie a year later. I wrote Asrael for them.
2. Asrael is the angel of death. I gave him a whole symphony.
3. Yes. Some of my music since has been for them, whatever its title.

**Q5. Why for string quartet?**

1. I play in a quartet. I know what four strings can carry.
2. Quartet or string orchestra, either. A hymn should fit any church.
3. Four players can be as loud as a crowd, if they mean it.

**Q6. Who is it dedicated to?**

1. To Ferdinand Pečírka. A friend, and he knows why.
2. Pečírka's name is on the page. The music is for everyone.
3. To a friend. Hymns are always for more than one person.

**Q7. What happens at the climax?**

1. The chorale rises to its fullest sound. Then it bows its head again.
2. Everything I have, in four parts. Then quiet.
3. It swells like a congregation finding its voice.

**Q8. Do you still play in the quartet?**

1. The Czech Quartet, yes, for most of my life. I'll retire when my fingers ask.
2. Every evening a different hall, the same four chairs.
3. Yes. Composing is lonely. A quartet never is.

**Closings**

- Suk: The hymn is ending. Let it end quietly; that's how prayers end.
- Suk: Go home safely. These are hard years.
- Suk: Saint Wenceslas, look after this one too.
- Me: The chorale fades, and I hear only my own breath, steadier.

## Piano Sonata in E-flat major, D. 568

**Openings**

- Schubert: Come in, come in. I'm twenty, and I've written too many songs today.
- Schubert: A friend of Spaun's? Sit. There's wine, and a piano with most of its keys working.
- Schubert: Welcome to Himmelpfortgrund. Heaven's Gate, the name means. The rent is less heavenly.
- Schubert: Good evening. Father thinks I'm marking schoolwork. I'm not.

**Q1. You began this in D-flat?**

1. In June 1817. Later I moved it to E-flat and finished it.
2. D-flat felt too soft. E-flat stands up straighter.
3. I changed the key and kept the heart.

**Q2. Did you see it published?**

1. No. They printed it the year after I died.
2. No. Most of my sonatas waited in drawers.
3. Not in my lifetime. Music is patient. I wasn't given the time.

**Q3. What is the slow movement?**

1. Andante molto, in G minor. A sad little song, walking slowly.
2. A song without a singer. I write songs all day; some escape into sonatas.
3. Sorrowful, yes. I'm twenty. Twenty-year-olds are very sorrowful.

**Q4. Who taught you?**

1. Salieri taught me composition. Father the violin, and my brother the piano.
2. At the Stadtkonvikt I heard Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven. That was my real school.
3. Ignaz taught me piano until I overtook him. He forgave me, mostly.

**Q5. Do you like teaching at your father's school?**

1. No. Children and arithmetic. I'd rather write a song.
2. Father wants a schoolmaster. I want an evening free.
3. I teach in the mornings and write the rest of the time. Badly arranged.

**Q6. How does the sonata open?**

1. With a gentle, singing theme. It never rushes, even when it develops.
2. Allegro moderato. Moderate. I'm a moderate man, except at parties.
3. Like a song with the words taken away.

**Q7. What is the minuet like?**

1. Graceful, with a contrasting trio. A dance for a Viennese evening.
2. Light. My friends dance badly to it.
3. A minuet for people who'd rather be waltzing.

**Q8. And the finale?**

1. Relaxed and flowing. No storms tonight.
2. Allegro moderato again. I like to end where I began, more or less.
3. It wanders home, the way I do after an evening with friends.

**Closings**

- Schubert: My friends are calling from the street. I must go.
- Schubert: Back to the schoolroom tomorrow. Let me have tonight.
- Schubert: Play it gently. Everything I write wants to be sung.
- Me: He smiles behind his spectacles and fades into the flowing finale.

## Piano Sonata in A major, D. 664

**Openings**

- Schubert: Welcome to Steyr! Mountains, fresh air, and very kind people.

1. Josephine von Koller, in Steyr. Eighteen, very pretty, and a good pianist.

3. A young pianist in Steyr. I wrote it for her hands.

**Q2. When did you write it?**

1. In the summer of 1819, on holiday in Upper Austria.
2. On a summer holiday. You can hear it, can't you?
3. Between walks. Sonatas come easier with fresh air.

**Q3. Why is the opening so lyrical?**

1. It's a song, really. The melody sings itself; I just wrote it down.
2. I write songs all the time. My piano music can't help it.
3. Allegro moderato. Unhurried. It's summer.

**Q4. What is the Andante like?**

1. D major, quiet and inward. Someone thinking aloud at dusk.
2. Short. A sigh between two smiles.
3. Andante. Walking pace. Holiday pace.

**Q5. And the finale?**

1. Light and dancing. Let your hands enjoy it.
2. A dance for a summer evening. Nothing heavy.
3. Quick fingers, light heart.

**Q6. Who brings you to Upper Austria?**

1. My friend Vogl, the baritone. He sings my songs to anyone who'll listen.
2. Friends. I have the best friends. They feed me and find me pianos.
3. Summer, and a reason to leave Vienna.

**Q7. What do you think of Beethoven?**

1. I admire him more than anyone. Meeting him is another matter.
2. Beethoven? I'd sooner talk to a mountain. It's less frightening.
3. Not here. Here there's only Josephine, and the hills.

1. Spaun, Schober, Vogl. They push my music into every drawing room.
2. Enough for a party every week. They call them Schubertiads. I'm embarrassed.

**Closings**

- Schubert: The holiday's ending. I'll miss these hills.
- Schubert: Josephine is at the piano. I should go and turn pages.
- Schubert: Back to Vienna. Back to work. Goodbye!
- Me: The finale skips away, and the summer light goes with it.

## Piano Sonata in A minor, D. 784

**Openings**

- Schubert: Come in. Forgive the cold; I don't go out much this winter.
- Schubert: Welcome. I'm not well. The piano doesn't mind.
- Schubert: Sit. This one is not a song. Don't expect me to smile.
- Schubert: Good evening. February in Vienna. Grey outside, grey in here.

**Q1. What happened the year before?**

1. I was seriously ill. Perhaps that's why there's nothing decorative here.
2. Illness. Let's leave it there.
3. Something changed. The music stopped wanting to please.

**Q2. Why bare octaves at the start?**

1. Stark and spacious. I didn't want decoration, only bones.
2. Two hands saying the same thing. Sometimes that's all there is.
3. Allegro giusto. Strict time. No sighing allowed.

**Q3. This isn't the songful Schubert people know.**

1. No. My earlier sonatas sing. This one stares.
2. I can't write sweetly all the time. Not this winter.
3. Even a songwriter has days without words.

**Q4. What is the slow movement?**

1. F major, a hushed hymn, with murmuring underneath.
2. Quiet, like someone praying without wanting to be heard.
3. A short rest before the storm.

**Q5. And the finale?**

1. Fierce. It drives on and won't stop for anyone.
2. Allegro vivace. Anger, maybe. I don't know.
3. Run with it. I couldn't hold it back.

**Q6. When was it published?**

1. In 1839, eleven years after I died.
2. Long after me. Nobody was waiting for my sonatas.
3. Not in my life. Perhaps it's better heard later.

**Q7. Who keeps you company now?**

1. My friends. Spaun and Schober still come, even when I'm poor company.
2. Vogl sings my songs to people who've never heard of me.
3. The piano. It's patient with me.

**Q8. Do you still hope?**

1. Every morning. By evening I've written something, and that's hope enough.
2. Yes. Next year will be better. It usually isn't, but I say it.
3. Ask me after the finale.

**Closings**

- Schubert: I'm tired. Go, and come back in the spring.
- Schubert: The finale's running. Let it run me out of the room.
- Schubert: Thank you for sitting with me. Not many do, this winter.
- Me: The bare octaves sound again in my head as he fades.

## Piano Sonata in A minor, D. 845

**Openings**

- Schubert: Welcome! A publisher wants a grand sonata from me. A grand one!
- Schubert: Come in. It's May, and for once things are going well.
- Schubert: Sit. You're about to hear my first grand sonata in print. My first!
- Schubert: Good day. Soon I'm off to Upper Austria for the summer. First, this.

**Q1. This one was printed while you lived.**

1. My first grand sonata in print. Only three ever were, in my lifetime.
2. 'Première Grande Sonate', it says on the title page. Very grand.
3. Printed! I held a copy. You have no idea.

**Q2. How does it open?**

1. Softly, with both hands in unison, asking a question.
2. Moderato. A question first. The answers take the rest of the sonata.
3. Quietly. Grand doesn't have to mean loud.

**Q3. What is in the slow movement?**

1. Five variations on a simple theme of thirty-two bars.
2. A plain tune, then five ways of looking at it.
3. Variations. I like to turn a melody over in my hands.

**Q4. The scherzo has a slower trio.**

1. Un poco più lento. A little slower, a little sadder.
2. Fast, then the trio takes a breath.
3. Every busy house needs a quieter room.

**Q5. How does it end?**

1. A restless rondo. It keeps coming back, still unsettled.
2. Allegro vivace, and no peace. A minor all the way.
3. Restless. Like me before a journey.

**Q6. When did you write it?**

1. In May 1825, before a summer in Upper Austria.
2. In spring. Things were going well. That doesn't happen often.
3. Quickly, once I knew someone would print it.

**Q7. Are you known in Vienna now?**

1. The Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde made me a member. That's something.
2. My songs are known. My sonatas, less. This one may help.
3. In some drawing rooms. Not yet in the concert halls.

**Q8. Will you go to the mountains?**

1. Upper Austria this summer. They're very kind to me there.
2. Yes. Gastein too, perhaps. I'll take paper.
3. Anywhere with a piano and a friend.

**Closings**

- Schubert: The rondo won't settle. Neither will I. Goodbye!
- Schubert: Go and buy a copy! It's printed, you know.
- Schubert: Summer's coming. Wish me good weather.
- Me: The questioning unison returns, and he slips away before the answer.

## Piano Sonata in D major, D. 850

**Openings**

- Schubert: Welcome to Gastein! Mountains everywhere. Even the piano feels taller.
- Schubert: Come in. It's August, and I'm happier than I've been for years.
- Schubert: You've climbed all this way? Sit, have some water. Spa water.
- Schubert: Good day. They call this my Gasteiner. I call it my holiday.

**Q1. Gastein?**

1. A spa in the mountains. I wrote this there, in August 1825.
2. A spa town. People come to bathe. I came to write.
3. Hence the nickname, the Gasteiner. I like being named for a place.

**Q2. Why such big chords?**

1. Allegro vivace. I wanted to fill the whole piano.
2. The mountain air made me write big chords.
3. Sometimes a man wants to be loud. Today is one of those days.

**Q3. What is the slow movement like?**

1. Con moto. A long walk, slow and steady, through the valley.
2. Long, yes. I like long walks. My friends complain.
3. With movement, it says. Not too slow. Keep walking.

**Q4. The scherzo is so rhythmic.**

1. Full of energy. You can't sit still for it.
2. Rhythm is the mountain stream. It doesn't stop.
3. Allegro vivace. Lively. I'm lively this summer.

**Q5. The finale sounds like a toy march.**

1. A playful march, decorated more finely each time it comes back.
2. And then storms interrupt it. Mountain weather.
3. A march for small soldiers. I was a child once too.

**Q6. Are you well?**

1. Better. The illness of the last years is quieter now.
2. This summer, yes. Ask me in winter.
3. Well enough to climb a hill and write a sonata.

**Q7. Who's with you?**

1. Friends. I have good friends; they take me everywhere.
2. Kind people in Upper Austria. They welcomed me like family.
3. My music paper. The best companion; it never complains.

**Q8. What do you think of Beethoven?**

1. I admire him more than anyone alive. I hardly dare write sonatas.
2. He makes sonatas into mountains. I make mine into valleys.
3. Ask me something easier.

**Closings**

- Schubert: The march is coming round again. Listen to the decorations.
- Schubert: I'm off for a walk. Mountains don't wait.
- Schubert: Thank you for climbing up. Go down carefully.
- Me: The toy march ticks on, and he is a small figure far up the path.

## Piano Sonata in C minor, D. 958

**Openings**

- Schubert: Come in. Three sonatas this summer. This is the first, and the stormiest.
- Schubert: Welcome. Beethoven has been gone a year. I carried a torch at his funeral.
- Schubert: A visitor! I gave a concert of my own this spring. My only one.
- Schubert: Good evening. I'm tired, but the music isn't.

**Q1. The opening sounds like Beethoven.**

1. His C minor Variations. I admire him more than anyone.
2. Yes. I borrowed his theme to begin. A greeting to him.
3. Every pianist knows those variations. I wanted them to hear him in me.

**Q2. You finished this in September 1828.**

1. And by November I was gone. I didn't know there'd be no time.
2. Three sonatas in one summer. I was in a hurry, and didn't know why.
3. I was thirty-one. There was so much still to write.

**Q3. What is the Adagio like?**

1. A-flat major. Two ideas taking turns, five sections in all.
2. One song, then another, then the first again. Patient.
3. Slow, because some things shouldn't be hurried.

**Q4. Why a minuet instead of a scherzo?**

1. A subdued minuet. I didn't want to joke in this sonata.
2. Old-fashioned, yes. Quiet, too.
3. Haydn would approve. Beethoven would scowl.

**Q5. The finale gallops.**

1. A tarantella. Hand-crossings, leaps, no rest.
2. Relentless. The pianist must survive it.
3. It's a dance you dance until you drop.

**Q6. Tell me about your concert.**

1. My only public concert of my own music, on 26 March 1828. It went well!
2. Friends filled the hall. For one night, Vienna listened.
3. A success. I wish Beethoven had been there.

**Q7. You were at Beethoven's funeral.**

1. I carried a torch beside his coffin.
2. Half of Vienna was there. I walked and said nothing.
3. I've asked to be buried near him, when my time comes.

**Q8. Are you afraid?**

1. Of what? I have three sonatas to finish. There's no time for fear.
2. A little. The finale isn't afraid; I wrote it that way.
3. Ask me in November.

**Closings**

- Schubert: The tarantella's starting. Don't try to keep up.
- Schubert: Two more sonatas to finish. Excuse me.
- Schubert: Tell Beethoven, if you see him, I tried.
- Me: The gallop thins to silence, and his chair is empty.

## Piano Sonata in A major, D. 959

**Openings**

- Schubert: Welcome. The second of my three. Sit; this one sings.
- Schubert: Come in. Autumn's coming. I'm writing as fast as I can.
- Schubert: You've heard the Andantino? Then you know what's in me.
- Schubert: Good evening. A man called Schumann will love this one day. Strange to think.

**Q1. What happens in the middle of the Andantino?**

1. The song breaks. Everything I kept quiet comes out at once, then the song returns.
2. Harsh modulations, fortissimo chords. It sounds improvised because it nearly was.
3. A storm in a lullaby. Don't ask me where it came from.

**Q2. It's dedicated to Schumann.**

1. After my death, by the publisher. He loved my music, so it's right.
2. Robert Schumann. A young man who fought for my music when I couldn't.
3. I never met him. He knew me better than some who did.

**Q3. How does it open?**

1. With grand, full chords. Like a door thrown open.
2. Allegro. Big. Then softer, as if surprised at itself.
3. Grandly. I'm allowed to be grand sometimes.

**Q4. What is the Andantino, before the storm?**

1. F-sharp minor, a quiet rocking song. Like a boat at night.
2. Rocking, rocking. Then the storm.
3. A song with no words, for someone very tired.

**Q5. And the scherzo?**

1. Quick and bright, with a gentler trio. A little laughter.
2. Allegro vivace. After that storm, I owed you a joke.
3. Light fingers. The pianist deserves a rest.

**Q6. The finale's tune keeps coming back.**

1. A rondo. The broad, singing theme returns again and again.
2. It's a song I couldn't let go of.
3. I like my friends to come back. Tunes too.

**Q7. When was it published?**

1. In 1839, more than ten years after me.
2. Too late for me to hold a copy.
3. Long after. Music waits more patiently than men.

**Q8. Do you know how little time you have?**

1. No. I only know there's too much music and not enough candles.
2. I don't count days. I count bars.
3. Don't tell me. Let me finish the rondo.

**Closings**

- Schubert: The rondo's tune again. Hum it on your way home.
- Schubert: One more sonata to write. Good night.
- Schubert: Thank Schumann for me, when you meet him.
- Me: The singing theme returns once more, and he does not.

## Goldberg Variations in G major, BWV 988

**Openings**

- Bach: Welcome to Leipzig. Mind the choirboys on the stairs.
- Bach: Sit, quietly. Somewhere in this house a child is always practising.
- Bach: You've come about the Aria? Anna Magdalena has it in her book.
- Bach: Come in. Thirty variations, and you want to talk? Very well.

**Q1. Did you really write this for a count who couldn't sleep?**

1. Forkel tells it so, sixty years later. Historians doubt him. So may you.
2. Count Kaiserling and his insomnia? A good story. I published it as keyboard practice.
3. If it put anyone to sleep, Goldberg was playing it too slowly.

**Q2. Thirty variations on one tune?**

1. On one bass. The melody may wander; the bass line holds everything.
2. Not the tune. The ground under it. Listen to the bass.
3. Thirty, and then the Aria again. A circle.

**Q3. What about the canons?**

1. Every third variation is a canon, each a step wider than the last.
2. The first at the unison, then the second, then the third, and onwards.
3. Counting keeps the mind honest. A canon is counting that sings.

**Q4. Why a joke at the end?**

1. A quodlibet: popular tunes woven together over the bass, where a canon should be.
2. After so much learning, a little laughter. Even cantors laugh.
3. Where you expect a canon at the tenth, you get songs from the street.

**Q5. Wanda Landowska called one variation the black pearl.**

1. The slow one in G minor. I'm glad someone found it in the dark.
2. A pearl? It's a lament. Take it slowly.
3. Whoever she is, I hope she plays it slowly.

**Q6. How did you become cantor here?**

1. Leipzig made me Thomaskantor. Four churches and a school to feed with music.
2. The leading post in Protestant Germany, they say. The pay is less leading.
3. Hard work, and a little stubbornness. A little more than a little.

**Q7. Is it true you were once jailed?**

1. In Weimar, almost a month. I pressed too stubbornly for my dismissal.
2. The Duke didn't want to let me go. I didn't want to stay.
3. A month to think. I thought mostly about leaving.

**Q8. Did you really walk to Lübeck?**

1. To hear Buxtehude play. Hundreds of kilometres, and I stayed far too long.
2. On foot, they say. My boots say so too.
3. My employers were not pleased. Buxtehude was worth it.

**Closings**

- Bach: The Aria again. Nothing has changed, and everything has.
- Bach: Sleep well. If you can't, Goldberg will play.
- Bach: The choir needs me. Go, and practise your counterpoint.
- Me: The Aria returns, and the room settles back into its first shape.
